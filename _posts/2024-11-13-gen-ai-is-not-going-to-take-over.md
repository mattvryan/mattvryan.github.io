title: GenAI Is Not Going To Take Over
published: False
---
One of the major television ad campaigns I remember from my youth is the Pepsi Challenge.  In these commercials, Pepsi would set up blind taste tests wherein individuals would taste a sample of Coca-Cola and then Pepsi (or reversed) without knowing which was which, and then select the one they liked best.  The commercials proudly proclaimed the results:  In blind taste tests, customers preferred Pepsi over Coke.

Coca-Cola was pretty worried about this and even released "New Coke" which was meant to be an improvement on Pepsi.  Surprisingly, customer response to New Coke was pretty poor.  Execs were bemused - hadn't customers shown a preference for Pepsi over Coke?  Why didn't they prefer "New Coke" even more?

It turns out that both Pepsi and Coca-Cola had been fooled by something we might call "the law of first impressions."  Pepsi had taken the idea of Coke and had tried to improve upon it in specific ways.  The first impression was indeed accurate - people preferred Pepsi over Coke, initially.  However, about halfway or so through the six-pack many converts found that they were already getting tired of Pepsi's enhanced flavor profile and they were reverting back to Coke.  Coca-Cola dumped "New Coke" and re-emphasized "Classic Coke".  They continued to lead the market and have ever since.

The Pepsi Challenge exemplifies the difference between a strong initial impression and staying power.  Trying to quantify all the aspects of a successful thing and then improve upon each of them might give a better initial impression but it doesn't necessarily improve staying power.  Something about the combination of all the factors that make up Coca-Cola gives it staying power, and trying to improve upon each facet doesn't actually make it better.

Music is also like this.  Most modern music is written for strong first impressions.  The goal is to hook you and get you to spend a buck on a song or to add it to your Spotify playlist.  This is all very intentional for most artists.  They're trying to sell you Pepsi, not Coke.  They don't care if you are still listening to this song in 20 years or even 20 weeks from now.  They just want to get your attention now, because that's how they get paid.

The music doesn't last though.  After you hear most modern music a handful of times you are sick of it (for some artists, like Imagine Dragons, you are so sick of it by halfway through the first listen that you want to shove a fork in your ear just to stop listening, but most are at least somewhat more palatable).  Meanwhile, people just keep listening to Pink Floyd and Led Zeppelin over and over.

There is an art to the proper assembly of all the relevant components together that gives things staying power.  These are songs that reach your heart.  Someone is speaking directly to your soul when you listen to these songs.  You're hearing a message that something in you yearns to hear again and again, and each time you listen you hear that message.  Well-written songs, well performed, do that to you.

I experienced this a few weeks ago at the regional Bands of America competition.  My son's marching band was one of the smaller bands in this competition, and they were going against some rivals they'd competed against many times before.  So I'd heard many of these performances more than once.

I realized during this final competition of the year something remarkable:  I was not getting bored listening to our school's marching band.  I looked forward to watching and listening, every time.  Many of the competitor's selections were starting to bore me, however.  I realized that during our school's performance it would hit me right in the chest, behind the sternum.  Every time.  I experienced it in a way I didn't experience most of the others.  As a result, they were boring me.  But our school's performance kept hitting me emotionally, every time.  You never get bored of that experience.

And indeed, in the regional competition I found out later that an additional judging element, "Audience Impact", had been added to the judging sheets that did not exist in the prior competitions.  The judges noticed the difference too.  We beat all the other schools in our division, some of which we'd struggled to beat in previous weekends.  It was Pepsi vs. Classic Coke all over again.  Their initial impression may have been better, but you had to admit the one you wanted to watch again and again was our school's performance.

---

Everyone is worried these days about GenAI.  I wouldn't claim to be an expert on GenAI, but I do know enough about how it works to say this:  The best GenAI is ever going to be able to offer, when it comes to artistic things, is Pepsi.  That's not bad at all, but it'll never be the best.  And GenAI will never be the best either.

Here's why:  The way GenAI works is to try to quantify and measure what makes something "good", and then improve upon each element.  Change, measure, adjust, change, measure, adjust, etc.  AI has no brain, no creativity, no intrinsic sense of excellence.  It only can measure and change and see if that is better or worse, and make adjustments.  It works on feedback loops.

But feedback loops really only work well on first impressions, not long-term impressions.  And they only work when you can quantify and explain why something is better.  When "better" is felt, not measured, AI can't operate on that.

If you are making a career in an artistic field, you're worried about GenAI because you think maybe in the future you won't be needed anymore.  What you should be worried about is whether you are nurturing and developing your artistic capability.  Whether you make music, paint pictures, produce movies, design buildings, or whatever your creative field might be - if your career is all about efficiently doing all the standard, non-creative things, I hate to tell you but your days are numbered.  GenAI is probably going to replace you.  However, if you are creative, inventive, and artistic - if you are making things that connect with people in difficult-to-quantify ways - you are good.  If people are hearing your songs or looking at your paintings and having an emotional reaction, that's staying power right there.  GenAI will then be a tool for you - not a replacement, but an enabler.  You will be leveraging GenAI to help you offer even more of your unique craft to the world, and we'll all be the better for it.

To apply it to the music industry:  Alter Bridge, you are fine.  Imagine Dragons, you will soon be replaced.  Thank heaven.
